How to keep a headache diary your doctor can use

A diary is one of the most useful things you can bring to a headache appointment. It doesn’t have to be elaborate. A few lines after each attack, kept up for a month or two, tell a clinician far more than memory will.

3 minute read · Last updated November 2026

Sudden, severe or unusual headache? Get emergency help: call 911, 999, 112 or your local emergency number. See the emergency signs.

Why a diary helps

Headaches are hard to remember accurately. Weeks later, most of us can’t say how many we had, which ones were bad, or what was going on around them. A diary fixes that. It shows how often attacks happen, how long they last, when they tend to come and what seems to set them off, which is the information a doctor needs to work out what kind of headache you have and what might help.

It also helps you. Patterns that are invisible day to day often show up once the entries are side by side.

What to write down

Note thisWhy it’s useful
Date and time it startedShows how often attacks come, and whether they favor certain days or times.
How bad it was, from 0 to 10Lets you and your doctor compare attacks, and see whether things are getting better or worse.
How long it lastedLength is one of the clues to the type of headache.
Where it hurtOne side, both sides, behind an eye, the back of the head: all part of the picture.
Other symptomsNausea, sensitivity to light or sound, visual changes (aura), neck stiffness.
Possible triggersPoor sleep, stress, a missed meal, weather, your period. Note them even if you aren’t sure.
What you did, and what you tookWhat helped, what didn’t, and how often you needed to reach for pain relief.
Effect on your dayMissed work, a canceled plan, a day in bed. This is what tells a clinician how much it matters.

Keep it simple enough to survive

  • Write it down when it happens. Thirty seconds at the time beats a careful entry you never get around to.
  • A partial entry is fine. A date and a number is better than nothing, and you can add details later.
  • Use whatever you’ll actually use. A notebook by the bed, a notes app or a tracking app all work.
  • Count the good days too. Headache-free days are useful to know about as well.

How long to keep it up

Clinicians often ask for at least a month of entries, and some prefer longer, because patterns need several attacks to show.

Reading it back

Look for a few simple things: how many headache days you had each month, whether they cluster on weekends, workdays or around your period, what time of day they start, and whether anything repeats in the day or two before an attack. Be careful with single events. A trigger is more convincing when it shows up repeatedly.

Take it to your appointment
