Headaches and weather: what we know, and how to check for yourself

“I can feel a storm coming” is one of the most common things people with migraine say. Weather is often named as a trigger, and for some people it clearly seems to matter. The research, however, is less certain than the folklore.

2 minute read · Last updated November 2026

Sudden, severe or unusual headache? Get emergency help: call 911, 999, 112 or your local emergency number. See the emergency signs.

  2. What the research says

What people report

The weather factors most often mentioned are falling or changing barometric pressure, approaching storms, heat, high humidity, bright sun and glare, cold and strong wind. Some people say they can feel a change coming.

What the research says

Studies of weather and migraine don’t agree. Some find a link between changes in pressure or temperature and attacks. Others find a weak link or none. Where there’s an effect, it differs a lot from person to person: some people seem quite sensitive, while others show no pattern at all.

Part of the difficulty is that weather rarely arrives alone. A hot, stormy day can also mean worse sleep, glare, a skipped meal or too little water, each of which can be a trigger in its own right. Untangling them from the outside is hard.

How to check for yourself

  1. Record your headaches with the date, time and severity. A headache diary is enough.
  2. Note the weather on headache days, and ideally on a few headache-free days too, so you have something to compare with.
  3. Give it a few months, then look for the same pairing coming up repeatedly. A pairing that keeps repeating over many attacks is more convincing than one bad day. A diary can suggest a link, but it can’t prove one.
  4. Remember the other suspects. Check how you slept, whether you ate and drank, and how stressed you were on those days.

HeadWell can use local weather, including air pressure, in its daily forecast if you allow it to use your location. That’s optional.

If weather does seem to matter to you

You can’t change the weather, but you can look after the things you can change around it: keep your sleep and meals regular, drink enough water, wear sunglasses against glare, and go easy on yourself on days you expect to feel rough. If weather-related attacks are frequent or hard to manage, mention it to your doctor, who can talk through options.

Worrying about the weather can become a stress of its own. If you find yourself checking forecasts anxiously, that’s worth mentioning to your doctor too.
