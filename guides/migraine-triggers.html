Common migraine triggers, and how to find yours

Ask ten people with migraine what sets off their attacks and you’ll hear ten different answers. That’s normal. Triggers are personal, and the useful question isn’t “what are the usual triggers?” but “which ones apply to me?”

3 minute read · Last updated November 2026

What a trigger is, and what it is not

A trigger is something that can bring on an attack in a person who is already prone to migraine. It isn’t the cause of the condition. Triggers also work unevenly: the same trigger might set off an attack on Monday and do nothing on Thursday, and attacks often seem to need several things to stack up at once.

Triggers people report most often

  • Stress, and the let-down after it. Some people get attacks during a stressful stretch, others on the first quiet weekend afterwards.
  • Sleep changes. Too little, too much, or an irregular pattern.
  • Skipped meals and dehydration. Long gaps between meals and not drinking enough are common ones.
  • Hormonal changes. Attacks often cluster around periods, and can change during pregnancy and around menopause.
  • Bright or flickering light. Bright sun and glare are the ones people mention most.
  • Strong smells. Perfume, smoke, gasoline and cleaning products come up often.
  • Alcohol. Red wine is the one most often named, but other drinks can set off attacks too.
  • Certain foods and drinks. Some people name particular foods, but the evidence for most of them is limited. Changes in caffeine, either too much or going without, are more commonly reported.
  • Weather. Widely reported, but the research is mixed. See our guide on headaches and weather.
  • Intense physical exertion.

None of these is a rule. They’re a list of places to look.

The trap: signs that look like triggers

Attacks often begin hours or even a day before the pain does. During that early phase some people crave sweet foods, feel low or irritable, yawn constantly or notice a stiff neck. If you eat chocolate because you’re craving it, and the headache arrives later, it’s easy to decide the chocolate caused it. It may be that the attack had already started. This is one reason single events make poor evidence.

How to test a suspect fairly

  1. Record first, change nothing. Keep a diary for a few weeks and note your suspects on the days you have a headache and the days you don’t.
  2. Look for repeats. A link that shows up again and again is worth acting on. One coincidence isn’t.
  3. Change one thing at a time. If you try cutting something out, do it alone and give it a few weeks, so you can tell whether it made a difference.
  4. Don’t cut out lots of foods at once without advice from a clinician or dietitian. Restrictive diets have limited evidence behind them for most people and can leave you short of nutrients.

You do not have to avoid everything

Triggers you can’t avoid, such as stress or weather, are better handled with a plan from your doctor than with a shrinking life. Regular routines for sleep and meals are the most commonly suggested starting point. If you notice yourself giving up things you enjoy because you fear an attack, tell your doctor.

A written record is what makes all of this possible. Our guide on keeping a headache diary covers what to note.
