The stages of a migraine: prodrome, aura, headache and postdrome

A migraine attack is more than the headache. Many people go through up to four phases, and knowing what they look like can help you spot an attack earlier and describe it better to a doctor. Not everyone gets every phase, and the pattern can differ from one attack to the next.

Prodrome: the early warning

The prodrome is the quiet start. It can begin hours before the pain, or even a day or two. The signs are often subtle and easy to put down to a bad day: yawning more than usual, feeling irritable, wanting sweets, a stiff neck, difficulty concentrating. Some people never notice a prodrome at all. Others learn their own pattern so well that they can tell, a day ahead, that an attack is coming.

Not every early sign ends in a headache. That’s why it helps to note the sign and what happened next.

Aura: a temporary disturbance

Aura affects roughly one in four people with migraine. It builds gradually over several minutes and usually fades within an hour. Visual aura is the most common: a shimmering patch, zigzag lines or a blind spot that slowly spreads. Aura can also bring tingling or numbness, often spreading from the hand to the face, or temporary trouble finding words. It can come before the headache, with it, or occasionally without any headache at all.

Get emergency help if you have weakness, trouble speaking or loss of vision that’s sudden or new to you. These can be signs of a stroke, not a migraine. If an aura lasts longer than an hour, or is your first, get medical advice promptly.

Headache: the attack itself

This is the phase most people mean by “a migraine”. The pain is often throbbing, often on one side, usually moderate to severe, and made worse by moving about. It typically comes with nausea and with sensitivity to light, sound or smells. Left untreated, it lasts from 4 hours to 3 days.

Postdrome: the hangover

After the pain eases, many people feel wrung out for up to a day or two: tired, foggy, a bit tender in the head or neck, flat in mood. Some feel unusually well. It’s part of the attack, which is why it’s worth noting how long it takes you to feel like yourself again.

Why the stages matter

  • They help you recognize an attack early. Ask your doctor what they advise you to do when you notice the first signs.
  • They stop you blaming the wrong thing. A craving or a bad mood may be the start of the attack, not its cause.
  • They make you a better witness. “I get yawning and a stiff neck the day before, then flashing lights, then about eight hours of pain” is a description a clinician can work with.

During an attack, many people find it helps to rest somewhere quiet and dim and to keep drinking water. For anything more than that, ask your doctor what plan is right for you.
