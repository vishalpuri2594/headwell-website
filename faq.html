Headache and migraine: your questions answered

Answers to what people ask most about headaches, migraine, tracking and HeadWell. This is general information for adults, not medical advice.

Headaches and migraine: the basics

What is a migraine?

Migraine is a neurological condition, not just a bad headache. An attack usually brings a throbbing or pulsing pain, often on one side of the head, together with some mix of nausea, sensitivity to light, sound or smells, and tiredness. Left untreated, an attack typically lasts between 4 and 72 hours.

Migraine runs in families and it’s common: roughly one person in seven worldwide has it.

Sources: NINDS: Migraine, ICHD-3: Migraine without aura, The Migraine Trust: What is migraine?

How long does a migraine last?

The headache phase of an attack typically lasts between 4 and 72 hours if it isn’t treated. The whole episode can run longer: early warning signs can start hours, or a day or two, before the pain, and the tired, foggy aftermath can last up to a day or two. An aura, if you get one, usually lasts between 5 and 60 minutes.

See a doctor if an attack lasts more than 72 hours, or if an aura lasts longer than an hour.

Sources: ICHD-3: Migraine without aura, American Migraine Foundation: How long does a migraine attack last?, NHS: Migraine

What are the symptoms of a migraine?

The main symptom is a headache that’s often throbbing or pulsing, usually moderate to severe, often on one side, and often worse when you move about. Nausea and vomiting are common, along with sensitivity to light, sound or smells. Some people get an aura first, such as zigzag lines or tingling, and some notice warning signs hours or a day before the pain.

Tiredness, a stiff neck and trouble concentrating are also common, before, during or after the pain.

Sources: NHS: Migraine, NINDS: Migraine, ICHD-3: Migraine without aura, Mayo Clinic: Migraine

What is the difference between a headache and a migraine?

“Headache” is the everyday word for head pain. Migraine is one particular kind, with its own pattern: moderate to severe pain, often one-sided and throbbing, usually with nausea or sensitivity to light and sound, and often made worse by ordinary movement such as climbing stairs.

Tension-type headaches are far more common and are usually milder: a dull, tight pressure on both sides of the head, usually without nausea. Plenty of people have more than one type, which is one reason a written record of what each attack felt like is useful.

Sources: NHS: Headaches, NHS: Migraine, ICHD-3: Migraine without aura, ICHD-3: Tension-type headache

What are the stages of a migraine?

Many attacks unfold in up to four phases, and not everyone gets all of them:

  • Prodrome: hours, or a day or two, before the pain, with subtle changes such as yawning, mood shifts, food cravings, neck stiffness or tiredness.
  • Aura (some people only): usually 5 to 60 minutes of visual or other sensory changes.
  • Headache: the painful phase.
  • Postdrome: for up to a day or two afterwards, a drained, foggy feeling.

Our guide to the stages of a migraine goes into more detail.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: How long does a migraine attack last?, The Migraine Trust: Stages of a migraine attack, NHS: Migraine, ICHD-3: Migraine with aura

What is a migraine aura?

Aura is a temporary disturbance, most often visual, that comes before or alongside the headache in some people with migraine. Typical signs are shimmering or zigzag lines, blind spots, numbness or tingling in a hand or the face, or trouble finding words. It builds gradually and usually fades within an hour. Roughly one in four people with migraine have aura.

If you get weakness, trouble speaking or a loss of vision that’s sudden or new to you, get emergency help straight away. If an aura lasts longer than an hour, or it’s your first, get medical advice promptly.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: Migraine symptoms FAQ, NHS: Migraine, ICHD-3: Migraine with aura

Can you have a migraine without a headache?

Yes. Some people get an aura, such as visual changes or tingling, without a headache afterwards. Doctors call this typical aura without headache. It’s easy to mistake for something else, so if you get aura symptoms for the first time, or they last longer than an hour, get medical advice promptly. Sudden weakness, trouble speaking or loss of vision needs emergency help.

Sources: ICHD-3: Migraine with aura, NHS: Migraine

What causes migraine?

The full picture isn’t known. Migraine involves changes in how the brain and nerves process sensory signals, and genes play a part, which is why it often runs in families. Certain things can set off an attack in someone who is prone to them. Those are called triggers, but a trigger isn’t the cause of migraine itself.

Sources: NINDS: Migraine, Mayo Clinic: Migraine

Is it a sinus headache or a migraine?

A genuine sinus headache comes from a sinus infection and usually arrives with other signs: thick colored nasal discharge, a fever, a reduced sense of smell. Many headaches that people call “sinus headaches”, with facial pressure, a blocked nose or watery eyes, turn out to be migraine, because migraine can cause those symptoms too.

If you keep getting sinus-type headaches, it’s worth asking a doctor whether migraine fits better.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: Sinus headache, NHS: Sinusitis

What are tension-type and cluster headaches?

Tension-type headache is the most common type: a dull, pressing pain on both sides of the head, like a tight band, usually mild to moderate. It can last from about half an hour to several days.

Cluster headache is much rarer and far more severe: intense pain around one eye, lasting roughly 15 minutes to 3 hours, often several times a day for weeks, with a watery or red eye, a blocked or runny nostril on the same side, and restlessness. It needs a doctor’s care.

Sources: NHS: Tension-type headaches, NHS: Cluster headaches, ICHD-3: Tension-type headache, ICHD-3: Cluster headache

What is chronic migraine?

Doctors use “chronic migraine” for headaches on 15 or more days a month for more than three months, with migraine features on at least 8 of those days. That’s different from having several attacks a month. If your headaches are this frequent, it’s worth talking to a doctor or headache specialist about your options.

Sources: ICHD-3: Chronic migraine, American Migraine Foundation: How long does a migraine attack last?

Triggers, patterns and tracking

What are common migraine triggers?

People most often report stress, too little or too much sleep, skipped meals, hormonal changes around periods, bright or flickering light, strong smells, alcohol, certain foods and drinks, changes in caffeine, and weather changes.

Triggers are personal. What sets off an attack for one person does nothing for another, and a trigger doesn’t bring on an attack every time, so your own history is a better guide than a generic list. Our guide to common migraine triggers has more.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: Top 10 migraine triggers, NINDS: Migraine, Mayo Clinic: Migraine

How do I find my own triggers?

Keep a record over several weeks: when each headache started, how bad it was, how long it lasted, what you ate and did, and how you slept. Patterns tend to show up across several attacks, not one.

Be wary of blaming a single event. Cravings, yawning and mood changes can be early signs that an attack has already started, so the chocolate or the bad mood you blame may have come after the attack began, not before it.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: Headache journals, The Migraine Trust: Distinguishing what is and isn’t a migraine trigger

What is a headache diary, and why keep one?

A headache diary is a simple log of your attacks. Doctors ask for one because memory is unreliable and a diary shows how often attacks happen, when, what seems to set them off and what helps. A few weeks of notes can make the conversation at an appointment more useful. See our guide on keeping a headache diary.

Sources: NHS: Headaches, The Migraine Trust: Keeping a migraine diary, American Migraine Foundation: Headache journals

What should I write down about a headache?

At a minimum: the date and time, how bad the pain was (0 to 10), how long it lasted, where it hurt, other symptoms such as nausea, light sensitivity or aura, possible triggers, and what you took, if anything. Sleep, meals and, if it applies to you, where you’re in your cycle add useful context.

HeadWell covers all of these, and everything beyond the pain level is optional.

Sources: The Migraine Trust: Keeping a migraine diary, American Migraine Foundation: Headache journals, NHS: Headaches

Does the weather really trigger headaches?

Many people say so, and changes in barometric pressure are among the weather factors most often mentioned. The research is mixed, though: some studies find a link, others don’t, and people differ a great deal. If you suspect weather affects you, the way to find out is to track your own attacks alongside the conditions. Our guide on headaches and weather explains what’s known.

Sources: Mayo Clinic: Weather and migraine, Weather and migraine: a 2024 review (PubMed Central), Weather and migraine: a 2025 systematic review (PubMed Central)

Can your period trigger migraine?

For many women, yes. Attacks often cluster around the start of a period, which is thought to be linked to the fall in estrogen. Menstrual migraine is usually defined as attacks that begin from about two days before to three days after the first day of bleeding, in at least two out of three cycles.

Tracking your cycle next to your headaches makes the pattern visible, and it’s useful information for a doctor.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: Hormonal and menstrual migraine, ICHD-3: Pure menstrual migraine without aura, NHS: Migraine

What are early warning signs of a migraine?

Before the pain, some people notice yawning, mood changes, food cravings, neck stiffness, tiredness, trouble concentrating or extra sensitivity to light and sound. These can show up hours, or a day or two, before an attack. This early phase is called the prodrome.

Not every warning sign ends in a headache, which is why it helps to note both the sign and what happened next. Over time some people learn to recognize their own pattern.

Sources: American Migraine Foundation: Migraine symptoms FAQ, NHS: Migraine, ICHD-3: Migraine with aura, The Migraine Trust: Stages of a migraine attack

Is HeadWell free, and which phones does it run on?

Yes, HeadWell is free to download and use. It runs on iPhone and iPad (iOS 18.6 or later) from the App Store, and on Android phones (Android 8.0 or later) from Google Play.

How do I log a headache in HeadWell?

Tap the + button. Quick log takes a couple of taps: set the pain level, then tap where it hurts on the head map. The full log adds symptoms, triggers, early warning signs and notes, and everything in it except the pain level is optional.

What does HeadWell show me?

Your entries become a calendar of headache days, charts of how often attacks happen, a look at triggers and time of day, and a short summary of what your own log shows. The Home screen adds a forecast for the day, a few insight cards drawn from your log, and articles picked for you.

Forecasts are estimates from your own logged data and, if you allow it, local weather. They can be wrong, and they aren’t a medical diagnosis or treatment recommendation.

Can I track periods and life events?

Yes. Switch on cycle tracking in your profile and you can log periods (start date, flow and when it ended). You can also log life events such as travel, exams, deadlines, illness or a stressful stretch. They appear on the same calendar as your headaches, so you can see how they line up.

What are Action Plans?

After you log a full headache entry or an early warning sign, HeadWell can show a short list of practical options, such as a breathing session, gentle stretches or protecting your sleep. Each comes with a reason, and many carry a safety note. They’re suggestions, not instructions: you can choose “Not now” or “Not for me”, and some are prompts to raise something with your doctor.

On iPhone you can switch Action Plans off in Profile, under App.

What is the Journey screen?

Journey, in your Profile, is a progress view. It looks at how many headache days you’re having, how many good days you have had lately, and, if you have filled in the questionnaires, how much headaches affect daily life. It explains every number it shows, and a bad stretch doesn’t reset your progress.

Does HeadWell have dark mode and Attack Mode?

Yes. By default HeadWell follows your phone’s light or dark setting. You can also choose Light, Dark or Attack Mode yourself in Profile, under App, in Appearance. Attack Mode dims the screen for times when bright light is painful.

Is there a library of articles?

Yes. HeadWell has a library of articles on migraine, tension headache, treatment, prevention and lifestyle. On iPhone it holds hundreds of articles. Android has the original set of more than forty for now, and the full library arrives with version 2.0. You can browse by category, search, filter, and read offline once they have loaded.

Can I share a report with my doctor?

Yes. HeadWell can create a PDF report of your history. It opens with a summary (how often attacks happen and the triggers you log most), then your log, and it includes your MIDAS and HIT-6 questionnaire scores if you have completed them. The report doesn’t include your name or email, but it does include answers you have entered, such as your intake answers and notes, so look it over before you share it. You decide whether to share it, by printing it, saving it or sending it yourself.

Does HeadWell work offline?

Yes. You can log and look back at your history without a connection, and your entries sync the next time you’re online. Anything that depends on weather needs a connection.

Can I export my data?

Yes. In the app, go to Profile, then App, then Export Data. Choose CSV, a spreadsheet of your headache log, or JSON, a backup that also includes your periods and your MIDAS and HIT-6 results. The file is created on your phone and you choose where it goes.

Does HeadWell replace my doctor, or diagnose my headaches?

No. HeadWell is a self-tracking and education tool. It doesn’t diagnose, treat or prevent any condition and it isn’t a substitute for medical advice. Use it to keep better records and to have a more useful conversation with your clinician.

Your entries are kept on your phone and, when you’re signed in, synced to our servers so your history survives a lost phone and works on more than one device. Getting help

When should I see a doctor about my headaches?

See a doctor if your headaches are regular (for example more than once a week), more frequent or severe than usual for you, get in the way of daily life, or have changed in character. Also go if you need pain relief on more than about two days a week. A headache pattern that starts for the first time after the age of 50 should be checked by a doctor promptly. Bring your diary.

For anything sudden, severe or unusual, see the emergency signs below.

Sources: NHS: Migraine, NINDS: Headache, Mayo Clinic: Headache

Which headache symptoms are an emergency?

Get emergency help straight away (call 911, 999, 112 or your local emergency number) for:

  • a sudden, severe headache that reaches its worst within a minute (a “thunderclap” headache);
  • a headache with a high fever or a stiff neck, or with a rash that doesn’t fade when you press a glass against it;
  • a headache after a blow to the head;
  • a headache with confusion, severe drowsiness, weakness, numbness, trouble speaking, double vision or loss of vision, trouble walking or balancing, a seizure or fainting, when it’s sudden or new to you;
  • the worst headache of your life, or a sudden headache that feels completely different from your usual ones.

Get same-day medical advice (an urgent clinic or your local urgent-advice line) if a headache:

  • is worse after coughing, straining, bending or exercise;
  • comes with jaw pain when you chew, or a tender scalp, especially if you’re over 50;
  • comes with eye pain or vision problems;
  • is new during pregnancy or just after giving birth;
  • keeps getting steadily worse over days or weeks;
  • is new, and you have cancer, HIV or a weakened immune system;
  • belongs to a migraine attack that has lasted more than 72 hours, or comes with an aura that lasts more than an hour or is your first.

HeadWell is for recurring headaches. A sudden, severe or entirely new headache needs medical attention. These pages are written for adults: for a child with headaches, see a doctor, and get urgent help if a child’s headache comes with a rash, fever or drowsiness, or follows a head injury.

Sources: NHS: Headaches, NINDS: Headache, Mayo Clinic: Headache, ICHD-3: Primary thunderclap headache

Can taking pain relievers too often make headaches worse?

Yes. Using pain relievers or migraine medicines too often can itself cause more headaches, a pattern called medication-overuse headache. Doctors diagnose it when someone who already has frequent headaches (on 15 or more days a month) has used simple painkillers such as acetaminophen (paracetamol) or ibuprofen on 15 or more days a month, or triptans, opioids or combination painkillers on 10 or more days a month, for more than three months.

Those numbers are where doctors draw the line, not safe limits. Many doctors advise asking for help much earlier: if you need pain relief on more than about two days a week, speak to a doctor or pharmacist.

Talk to a doctor or pharmacist before you change or stop a medicine. Stopping the overused medicine is often part of the treatment, but headaches can get worse for a while first, and some medicines, such as codeine and other opioids, need to be cut down gradually with medical help.

Sources: ICHD-3: Medication-overuse headache, American Migraine Foundation: What is medication overuse headache?, The Migraine Trust: Q&A on medication-overuse headache, Medication-overuse headache guideline, 2022 (PubMed Central), NHS: Migraine
